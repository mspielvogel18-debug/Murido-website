Supporting a Grieving Loved One: A Guide for Family and Friends

The Murido TeamOct 11, 2026 · 5 min read

When someone you care about is grieving, it can be hard to know what to say or do. You may worry about saying the wrong thing, making them cry, or intruding. You may feel helpless, because you cannot fix their pain. So sometimes people say nothing at all, and the grieving person feels alone.

The good news is that you do not need perfect words to help. Most grieving people are not looking for advice or solutions. They are looking for presence, patience, and care. This guide offers practical, gentle ways to support someone through grief, from the first days to the months and years that follow.

The most important thing: show up

The single most helpful thing you can do is be there. That might mean visiting, calling, sending a message, or sitting with them in silence. It does not have to be complicated.

Many grieving people say that what hurt most was not what people said, but that people disappeared. Showing up, even awkwardly, says "You are not alone."

You cannot take their pain away. But you can make sure they do not carry it alone.

What to say

You do not need to say much. Simple, sincere words are often best.

Helpful things to say

  • "I am so sorry."
  • "I do not know what to say, but I am here."
  • "I have been thinking about you."
  • "I remember when [their loved one] ..." followed by a memory.
  • "There is no right way to feel."
  • "You do not have to talk, but I am here if you want to."
  • "Can I bring dinner on Tuesday?"

Things to avoid

Some common phrases, even when meant kindly, can hurt:

  • "They are in a better place."
  • "Everything happens for a reason."
  • "At least they lived a long life."
  • "I know exactly how you feel."
  • "You need to be strong."
  • "Time heals all wounds."
  • "Let me know if you need anything."

That last one seems helpful, but it puts the burden on the grieving person to ask. Specific offers tend to be much easier to accept.

What to do

Actions often speak louder than words in grief.

In the first days and weeks

  1. Bring food, but ask about preferences or dietary needs. Disposable containers save them from returning dishes.
  2. Help with practical tasks, like childcare, pet care, errands, or answering the door.
  3. Attend the funeral or memorial, if appropriate.
  4. Offer to make calls or handle logistics.
  5. Sit with them, even in silence.

In the following months

Grief often gets harder after the initial support fades. This is when your presence can matter most.

  • Check in regularly, with a text, call, or visit.
  • Invite them to things, even if they often decline. Keep inviting.
  • Help with ongoing tasks, like yard work, meals, or paperwork.
  • Remember important dates, like birthdays, anniversaries, and holidays.
  • Mention their loved one by name.

Our guide to beyond casseroles and checklists explores why ongoing support matters so much.

Saying their name

Many grieving people fear that their loved one will be forgotten. Saying the person's name, sharing a memory, or noting what they would have thought about something can be a powerful gift.

You might worry that mentioning the person will remind them of their loss. But they have not forgotten. Hearing their loved one's name often brings comfort, even if it also brings tears.

Listening well

When a grieving person wants to talk, the most helpful thing is to listen. Good listening in grief often means:

  • Letting them lead the conversation.
  • Not rushing to offer advice or silver linings.
  • Being comfortable with silence and tears.
  • Letting them tell the same story more than once.
  • Not comparing their loss to your own experiences unless they ask.

Sometimes the best response is simply, "That sounds so hard."

Understanding that grief is not linear

Grief does not follow a set timeline or order. Your loved one may seem to be doing well one day and struggling the next. They may laugh at a party and cry in the car on the way home. This is normal.

Try not to judge or rush their grief. Avoid saying things like "You should be feeling better by now." Our guide to beyond the five stages explains why grief rarely follows a neat path.

Supporting different kinds of loss

Some losses come with extra layers.

  • Suicide loss may involve shock, guilt, and stigma. Our guide to grief after suicide loss offers insight.
  • Pregnancy and infant loss may be less visible. Our guide to pregnancy and infant loss explains more.
  • Losing a partner can upend daily life entirely.
  • Disenfranchised losses, like the death of an ex or a pet, may not receive much recognition.

Whatever the loss, your support matters.

Supporting grieving children

Children grieve differently from adults, and their grief may come and go quickly. They may seem fine one moment and very upset the next. You can help by:

  • Answering questions honestly and simply.
  • Keeping routines steady.
  • Letting them express feelings through play or art.
  • Including them in rituals, if appropriate.
  • Being patient with changes in behavior.

Taking care of yourself

Supporting someone through grief can be emotionally demanding, especially if you are also grieving. Make sure to care for yourself, too. Take breaks, lean on your own support system, and remember that you do not have to be everything to the grieving person.

When to encourage more help

Sometimes grief becomes overwhelming. You might gently encourage your loved one to seek professional support if they:

  • Seem unable to function in daily life for a long time.
  • Talk about feeling hopeless or not wanting to live.
  • Are drinking heavily or using substances.
  • Have stopped caring for themselves or others.

If they talk about suicide, take it seriously. Stay with them, encourage them to call or text 988 in the United States, and help them get immediate support.

Your presence matters

You do not need special training or perfect words to support a grieving person. What matters is that you show up, keep showing up, and let them grieve in their own way.

Your care, patience, and presence can make a real difference. Long after the funeral, a simple text that says "I am thinking of you and of them today" may be one of the most meaningful gifts you ever give.
