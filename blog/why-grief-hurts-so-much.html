Why Grief Hurts So Much: The Science of Attachment and Loss

The Murido TeamJun 1, 2026 · 3 min read

Grief can feel physically unbearable, and that is not your imagination. Losing someone close affects the same systems that kept humans alive for most of our history: our bonds with each other.

Attachment is built into us

From infancy, humans form deep attachments to the people who care for them and whom they care for. These bonds shape how we feel safe, how we regulate stress, and even how our bodies function. When someone we are attached to dies, that whole system loses its anchor.

The brain keeps looking for them

Researchers who study grief, such as Mary-Frances O'Connor, describe the brain as holding two conflicting pieces of information: they are gone, and they are supposed to be here. Your mind built a map of the world with that person in it. Updating that map is slow work. In the meantime, you may expect them to walk in, reach for your phone to call them, or feel a jolt each time you remember.

Why it feels physical

Social pain and physical pain share some of the same pathways in the brain. Stress hormones rise after a loss, which can affect sleep, appetite, immunity, and heart rhythm. In rare cases, severe emotional stress can trigger a temporary heart condition sometimes called broken heart syndrome. The ache in your chest is real.

Why some losses hurt more

The intensity of grief often reflects the depth and daily presence of the relationship, how sudden or traumatic the death was, and how much of your identity and routine involved the person. Losing someone you saw every day can upend countless small habits at once.

How the hurt changes

The brain does learn, slowly. Over time, it builds a new understanding that includes the loss. That learning happens partly through living: going through days, seasons, and anniversaries without the person, and finding ways to keep them with you. This is why grief often softens over time without the love disappearing.

What this means for you

If grief feels enormous, it is because the bond was enormous. Be patient with your brain as it relearns the world. Support your body with rest, food, and gentle movement. And reach out if pain becomes too much to carry alone. In the US, call or text 988 any time.
