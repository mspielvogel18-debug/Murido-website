Grief Counseling: What It Is and What to Expect

The Murido TeamSep 2, 2026 · 6 min read

Many people think about grief counseling but are not sure whether it is for them. Maybe you wonder if your grief is "bad enough." Maybe you worry it will mean reliving painful memories. Maybe you simply do not know what happens in a session or how to find someone.

This guide explains what grief counseling is, who it can help, what usually happens in sessions, and how to find a counselor who feels like a good fit. It is meant to make the idea less mysterious, so you can decide whether it might help you.

What grief counseling is

Grief counseling is support from a trained professional who helps you cope with the death of someone you love. It can take place one on one, with a family, or in a group. Sessions may be in person, by video, or over the phone.

Grief counselors come from different backgrounds. Some are licensed therapists, counselors, or social workers who specialize in grief. Others work through hospices, hospitals, funeral homes, or community organizations. Some faith communities also offer grief counseling through trained chaplains or pastoral counselors.

The goal of grief counseling is not to make grief go away. It is to help you understand what you are feeling, find ways to cope, and carry the loss in a way that allows you to keep living.

Counseling is not about fixing your grief. It is about having someone walk alongside you while you carry it.

Who grief counseling can help

You do not need to be in crisis to benefit from grief counseling. Many people seek support simply because grief feels heavy and they want a place to talk. Counseling may be especially helpful if:

  • You feel stuck, or your grief is not softening over time.
  • The death was sudden, violent, or traumatic.
  • You lost someone to suicide or overdose.
  • You are struggling with intense guilt or anger.
  • You have intrusive memories or flashbacks.
  • You are dealing with several losses at once.
  • Your support network is small or not understanding.
  • Grief is affecting your work, relationships, or health.
  • You are having thoughts of not wanting to live.

If your grief has been very intense for a long time, our guide to prolonged grief explains when extra support can help.

Grief counseling versus grief therapy

You may hear the terms "grief counseling" and "grief therapy" used differently.

  1. Grief counseling often refers to support for typical grief: helping people express feelings, understand the process, and cope with daily life.
  2. Grief therapy often refers to more intensive treatment, for grief that is complicated by trauma, depression, anxiety, or prolonged distress.

In practice, the lines can blur. Many therapists offer both. What matters most is finding someone who understands grief and can respond to what you need.

What happens in a first session

The first session is usually about getting to know each other. Your counselor may ask:

  • Who died, and what was your relationship like?
  • How did they die?
  • How have you been feeling since the loss?
  • What support do you have?
  • How are you sleeping, eating, and managing daily life?
  • What would you like to get out of counseling?

You do not have to share everything at once. You can tell the counselor if certain topics feel too hard to talk about yet. A good counselor will move at your pace.

What sessions might include

Grief counseling can look different depending on the counselor and your needs. Sessions often include some of the following.

Talking about the person

Many sessions focus on remembering the person who died. You might share stories, look at photos, or talk about what they meant to you. This can be painful, but also comforting.

Exploring feelings

Counselors can help you name and understand feelings like guilt, anger, relief, or numbness. They can help you see that these feelings are normal parts of grief.

Working through the death itself

If the death was traumatic, a counselor may help you process what happened. This might include talking through the events or using specific techniques for trauma.

Coping skills

You may learn tools for managing waves of grief, intrusive thoughts, sleep problems, or anxiety. These might include breathing practices, journaling, or mindfulness. Our guide to mindful grieving gives a taste of some of these.

Continuing the bond

Many counselors help people find healthy ways to stay connected to their loved one, such as rituals, letters, or carrying their values forward. You can read more about continuing bonds.

Adjusting to life after loss

Counseling may also focus on practical changes: new roles, decisions, relationships, and finding meaning again.

Approaches counselors may use

Grief counselors draw on many different approaches. Some you might hear about include:

  • Supportive counseling, which focuses on listening and validation.
  • Cognitive behavioral approaches, which explore how thoughts affect feelings.
  • Acceptance based approaches, which focus on making room for painful feelings and acting on your values.
  • Trauma focused therapies, for losses that involved trauma.
  • Narrative approaches, which help you tell and reshape the story of your loss.
  • Specialized grief treatments designed for prolonged grief.

You do not need to know which approach is best. It is fine to ask a counselor how they work and why.

How long counseling lasts

There is no set length for grief counseling. Some people find a handful of sessions enough to feel steadier. Others continue for months or longer. Many people come back to counseling at different points, such as around an anniversary or after another loss.

You can talk with your counselor about how things are going and adjust as needed.

Finding a grief counselor

There are several ways to find a counselor who specializes in grief:

  1. Ask your doctor for a referral.
  2. Contact a local hospice. Many offer bereavement counseling, sometimes even if your loved one was not in their care.
  3. Search therapist directories and filter for grief or bereavement.
  4. Ask your faith community.
  5. Check with your workplace about employee assistance programs.

When you contact a counselor, you might ask:

  • Do you have experience with grief and loss?
  • Have you worked with people who experienced this kind of loss?
  • How do you usually work with grieving clients?
  • What are your fees, and do you take my insurance?

Finding the right fit

The relationship between you and your counselor matters a lot. You should feel heard, respected, and safe. It is normal to feel nervous at first. But if, after a few sessions, you do not feel comfortable, it is okay to look for someone else. Changing counselors is common and is not a failure.

When counseling feels hard

Counseling can sometimes bring up strong emotions. You may leave a session feeling raw or tired. This is often part of the process. It can help to plan something gentle after sessions, like a walk or a quiet evening.

If counseling consistently leaves you feeling worse, talk with your counselor about it. They can adjust their approach or help you find a better fit.

A place to bring your grief

Grief counseling is not for people who are grieving wrong. It is for anyone who would like support carrying a heavy loss. It offers a space where you can say anything, feel anything, and be met with understanding.

If you are unsure, you might start with a single session to see how it feels. Our overview of grief support options also covers other paths, like groups and peer support. Whatever you choose, you deserve support that fits you and your grief.
