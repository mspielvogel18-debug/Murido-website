Willingness vs. Willpower: Why You Can't Force Your Way Through Grief

The Murido TeamMay 11, 2026 · 5 min read

Many of us were raised to handle hard things with willpower. Grit your teeth. Push through. Keep it together. That approach works for some challenges, like finishing a marathon or studying for an exam. Grief is different. The harder you try to force your way through it, the more it seems to dig in.

This guide looks at a gentler alternative: willingness. Not willingness to suffer for its own sake, but willingness to let grief be present while you keep living. It sounds small. For many people, it changes everything.

What willpower looks like in grief

Willpower in grief usually means trying to control the feelings. It can look like:

  • Refusing to cry in front of anyone
  • Staying constantly busy so there is no time to feel
  • Telling yourself you should be over this by now
  • Avoiding photos, places, or conversations that might stir things up
  • Forcing yourself to be "strong" for others
  • Setting a mental deadline for when the grief should end

None of these come from a bad place. They usually come from love, responsibility, or fear of falling apart. But they share an assumption: that grief is an enemy to defeat.

Why forcing it tends to backfire

Grief is not a task with a finish line. It is the ongoing response of a person who has lost someone they love. Trying to overpower it is a little like trying to hold a beach ball underwater. You can do it for a while, and it takes a lot of effort. Eventually your arms get tired, and the ball shoots up, often at the worst possible moment.

When grief is pushed away constantly, people often notice:

  1. Exhaustion. Holding feelings down takes enormous energy.
  2. Sudden floods. Emotions break through in unexpected places, like the office or the checkout line.
  3. Numbness. Pushing down the painful feelings tends to push down the good ones too.
  4. Physical tension. Grief that is not felt often settles in the shoulders, jaw, and stomach.
  5. A sense of being stuck. The grief does not move because it is never allowed to.

You cannot force grief to finish. But you can stop fighting it long enough for it to start changing.

What willingness looks like

Willingness means making room. It means saying, in effect, "This hurts, and I am willing to feel it, because it is part of loving them." It is an attitude of openness rather than resistance.

Willingness is not:

  • Giving up
  • Wallowing or letting grief take over every hour
  • Liking the pain
  • Approving of the loss

Willingness is:

  • Letting tears come when they come
  • Allowing a memory to sit with you for a moment instead of shoving it away
  • Saying their name, even when your voice shakes
  • Going to the places that matter to you, at your own pace
  • Accepting that some days will be harder than others

This idea draws on acceptance-based approaches in psychology, which suggest that our struggle against painful feelings often causes more suffering than the feelings themselves. We look at that more in Beyond Fighting Grief: Why Leaning In Helps.

A small practice in willingness

Next time grief arrives, you could try this short exercise. It takes a minute or two.

1. Pause

Stop what you are doing, if you can. Notice that grief is here.

2. Locate it

Where is it in your body? A heavy chest, a tight throat, a hollow stomach? Simply notice.

3. Breathe into it

Imagine your breath moving toward that place. You are not trying to make the feeling go away. You are making room around it.

4. Say something kind

Try a phrase like "This is grief. It makes sense that I feel this. I loved them."

5. Choose your next step

When you are ready, return to your day, or let yourself rest if that is what you need.

Practices like this, repeated over time, can help grief feel less like an ambush and more like a visitor you know how to greet.

Where willpower still belongs

Willpower is not useless in grief. It just has a different job. It can help you get out of bed, eat something, return a phone call, or show up for a child's school play when every part of you wants to stay home. Willpower is good at actions. It is poor at feelings.

A helpful way to think about it: use willpower for what you do, and willingness for what you feel. You can choose to take a walk even when you are heavy with sadness, and you can let the sadness come along on the walk. You do not have to wait for the feeling to leave before you live your life, and you do not have to get rid of it to keep moving.

Willingness and the people around you

Many grieving people use willpower for the sake of others. A parent may hold it together for their children. A partner may try to stay strong for the rest of the family.

There is love in that. There is also a cost. Children and loved ones often learn more from seeing grief handled openly than from seeing it hidden. Saying "I am sad today because I miss Grandpa" can teach a child that grief is normal and survivable. It gives others permission to grieve too.

You do not have to fall apart in front of everyone. But you also do not have to pretend.

Willingness when you do not feel ready

Some days, even willingness feels like too much. That is fine. Willingness can start very small:

  • Being willing to look at one photo
  • Being willing to listen to one song
  • Being willing to tell one person how you are really doing
  • Being willing to cry in the shower

Small acts of willingness add up. They teach your mind that grief can be felt and survived.

What changes over time

People who practice willingness often notice that grief does not vanish, but it changes. The waves still come, but they may pass more quickly. Memories may start to bring warmth along with the ache. There is more space for other parts of life alongside the loss.

This is not because the love has faded. It is because the grief is no longer being forced underground. It has room to move, and grief that can move tends to soften. Our guide on Will It Ever Stop Hurting? talks more about how that softening tends to happen.

When willingness is not enough on its own

Willingness is a helpful approach, but it is not a cure for every kind of pain. If grief has left you unable to function for a long time, if you are relying on alcohol or other substances, or if you are having thoughts of ending your life, it is important to get more support. A grief counselor or therapist can help.

If you are thinking about harming yourself, please call or text 988 now to reach the Suicide and Crisis Lifeline.

Letting go of the fight

You have probably been strong in more ways than anyone knows. Grief is not asking you to be strong in the old way. It is asking for something softer, and in some ways harder: to let yourself feel what you feel, without a battle. That is not weakness. It may be the bravest thing you do.
