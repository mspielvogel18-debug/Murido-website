Coping With Grief: A Practical Guide

The Murido TeamOct 23, 2026 · 5 min read

When grief arrives, it can upend everything. Daily routines fall apart. Simple tasks feel impossible. Emotions come in waves you cannot predict. You may wonder how you are supposed to keep going, let alone feel okay again.

This guide offers practical, gentle ways to cope with grief day to day. It is not a set of rules. Grief is personal, and what helps one person may not help another. Think of this as a toolbox. Take what is useful and leave the rest.

First, a reminder

There is no right way to grieve. Grief does not follow stages or timelines. It can include sadness, anger, guilt, relief, numbness, and even moments of joy. All of these are normal. Our guide to what grief is explains more about the many forms grief can take.

Coping does not mean not hurting. It means finding ways to keep living while you hurt.

Caring for your body

Grief is not only emotional. It affects the body too. Taking care of basic needs can make a real difference in how you cope.

Eat something, regularly

Many grieving people lose their appetite or forget to eat. Try to eat small amounts at regular times, even if you do not feel hungry. Simple foods are fine. Accept meals from friends if offered.

Protect sleep

Grief often disrupts sleep. A few things that may help:

  1. Keep a consistent bedtime and wake time.
  2. Create a calming evening routine.
  3. Limit screens before bed.
  4. Avoid caffeine and alcohol late in the day.
  5. If you cannot sleep, get up and do something quiet, then try again.

Move your body

Gentle movement can ease tension and lift mood. A short walk, stretching, or yoga can help. You do not need intense exercise.

Get outside

Even a few minutes of daylight and fresh air can help. Many people find nature especially soothing.

Limit alcohol

Alcohol can numb pain temporarily but often deepens low moods and disrupts sleep.

Our guide to the physical side of grief explores how grief affects the body.

Caring for your emotions

Let feelings come

Allowing yourself to cry, feel angry, or feel sad can help grief move. Fighting feelings often makes them stronger. Our guide to beyond fighting grief explains why.

Name what you feel

Saying "This is sadness" or "This is guilt" can make feelings feel more manageable.

Ride the waves

Grief often comes in waves. When one arrives, breathe slowly, ground yourself, and remind yourself it will pass. Our guide to grief waves offers more.

Be kind to yourself

Treat yourself as you would a grieving friend. Our guide to self compassion in grief has simple practices.

Staying connected

Lean on others

Let friends and family help. Be specific about what you need. "Can you come over Saturday?" is easier for people to say yes to than "Let me know if you need anything."

Find people who understand

Support groups can connect you with others who have experienced similar losses. Many people find this especially comforting.

Stay connected to the person who died

Many people find comfort in continuing a bond with their loved one. You might talk to them, write letters, keep their belongings, or carry on their traditions. Our guide to continuing bonds explores this.

Managing daily life

Lower expectations

You may not be able to function as you did before. That is okay. Focus on what is essential and let the rest wait.

Make small lists

Write down one or two things to do each day. Crossing them off can bring a sense of accomplishment.

Delay big decisions

Many counselors suggest waiting before making major changes, like moving or selling a home, unless necessary. Our guide to making decisions after a loss can help.

Ask for practical help

Friends can help with meals, errands, childcare, yard work, or paperwork. Accepting help is not weakness.

Build gentle routines

Routines can provide stability. Even small anchors, like a morning cup of tea or an evening walk, can help structure your day.

Preparing for hard days

Plan for anniversaries and holidays

Birthdays, anniversaries, and holidays can be especially hard. Think ahead about how you want to spend these days. Our guide to the first time without them offers ideas.

Create rituals

Rituals can give grief a place to go. Lighting a candle, visiting a grave, or cooking their favorite meal can help. Our guide to grief rituals has many ideas.

Have a plan for overwhelming moments

Keep a short list of things that help when grief feels too much: breathing, calling a friend, stepping outside, or holding a comfort object. Our guide to when grief feels overwhelming has more.

Making space for grief

Set aside time to grieve

Some people find it helpful to set aside a short time each day for grief, such as looking at photos or writing. This can help grief feel less like it is ambushing you all day.

Write

Journaling or writing letters can help untangle thoughts and feelings, and preserve memories.

Create

Art, music, crafts, or cooking can give grief a form when words are hard to find.

Practice mindfulness

Mindfulness can help you stay present with grief without being swept away. Our guide to mindful grieving offers simple practices.

Watching for warning signs

Grief is not a mental illness. But sometimes it overlaps with depression, anxiety, or prolonged grief. Consider reaching out for professional help if you notice:

  • Hopelessness most of the time.
  • Being unable to manage daily tasks for a long period.
  • Using alcohol or substances to cope.
  • Intense guilt or intrusive memories that do not ease.
  • Thoughts of not wanting to live.

If you are having thoughts of suicide, please reach out right away. In the United States, call or text 988.

Getting support

There are many forms of grief support: counseling, support groups, faith communities, online resources, and apps. Our guide to grief support options explains each one. You do not have to choose just one. Many people find a mix works best.

One day at a time

Coping with grief is not about getting it right. It is about getting through, one day, one hour, sometimes one breath at a time. Some days will feel manageable. Others will not. Both are part of grieving.

Over time, many people find that grief becomes easier to carry. It does not disappear. But life slowly grows around it, making room for connection, meaning, and moments of peace. You do not have to get there today. You only have to take the next small step.
