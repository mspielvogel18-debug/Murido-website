Disenfranchised Grief: When Your Loss Isn't Recognized

July 16, 2026 · 3 min read · The Murido Team

Grief researcher Kenneth Doka coined the term disenfranchised grief to describe losses that are not openly acknowledged, socially supported, or publicly mourned. If you have ever felt you were not allowed to grieve, this may be what you are feeling.

Examples

  • The death of an ex-partner or estranged family member
  • A miscarriage, stillbirth, or failed adoption
  • The death of a pet
  • The death of a friend, coworker, or online friend
  • A relationship others did not know about or approve of
  • A death by suicide or overdose, where stigma silences people
  • Losing someone to dementia while they are still alive

Why it hurts more

When others do not recognize your loss, you may not get time off, condolences, or a place at the funeral. You may feel you have to hide your grief or justify it. That isolation can make grief heavier and longer.

Your grief is valid

Grief does not require permission. If you loved, needed, or were connected to someone or something, the loss is real. You do not have to rank it against anyone else's.

Ways to honor a disenfranchised loss

  • Create your own ritual: light a candle, plant something, write a letter.
  • Tell one trusted person the full story.
  • Find a group for your specific loss. Many exist for pregnancy loss, pet loss, and suicide loss.
  • Keep memories somewhere private if you cannot share them publicly.

When others minimize it

You might say: "It may not seem big to you, but it was a big loss for me." You do not owe anyone an explanation, but sometimes naming it helps others understand.

Getting support

Grief counselors understand disenfranchised grief well and can offer validation others may not. If isolation becomes overwhelming or you feel unsafe, call or text 988.
