Kinds of Loss

Disenfranchised Grief: When Your Loss Isn't Recognized

The Murido TeamJul 16, 2026 · 5 min read

Some losses are met with flowers, casseroles, and time off work. Others are met with silence. If you are grieving a loss that others do not seem to recognize, you may feel as though your pain does not count, or that you have to hide it. That experience has a name: disenfranchised grief.

This guide explains what disenfranchised grief is, the kinds of losses that often fall into it, why it can be so isolating, and what can help you grieve openly, even when others do not understand.

What disenfranchised grief means

Disenfranchised grief is grief that is not openly acknowledged, socially supported, or publicly mourned. It is not that the loss is small. It is that the people and systems around you do not treat it as worthy of grief.

When grief is disenfranchised, you might hear things like:

  • "It was only a dog."
  • "You were not even that close."
  • "At least it was early in the pregnancy."
  • "You two were divorced anyway."
  • "It has been long enough."

Or you might hear nothing at all, which can feel even worse.

Your grief does not need anyone's permission to be real.

Losses that often go unrecognized

Relationships others do not see as primary

  • The death of an ex-partner
  • The death of a friend, coworker, or mentor
  • The death of a partner in a relationship that was hidden or not legally recognized
  • The death of a stepparent, in-law, or chosen family member

Losses tied to stigma

  • Death by suicide or overdose
  • Death related to an illness that carries stigma
  • Death of someone who was incarcerated

Losses that are not deaths

  • Divorce or a breakup
  • Estrangement from family
  • Infertility
  • Losing a job or career
  • Losing a home
  • A loved one's dementia or major personality change

Losses others minimize

  • Pregnancy loss, including miscarriage and stillbirth. See Pregnancy and Infant Loss.
  • The death of a pet
  • The death of someone very old, as if age makes it easier

Grief that is judged as wrong

  • Grief that lasts longer than others expect
  • Grief expressed in ways others find uncomfortable
  • Grief from people others assume do not grieve, like young children or people with cognitive disabilities

Why disenfranchised grief can be harder

When a loss is not recognized, grieving people often:

  1. Lack support. There may be no time off work, no gatherings, no cards.
  2. Feel shame. They may wonder whether they are overreacting.
  3. Hide their grief. Grief that is hidden often becomes heavier.
  4. Miss rituals. There may be no funeral, or they may not be welcome at one.
  5. Feel alone. Without others who acknowledge the loss, isolation can deepen.

Over time, disenfranchised grief can become stuck. That is not a personal failing. It is what happens when grief has nowhere to go. See Is It Grief or Something More?.

Validating your own grief

When others do not recognize your loss, it can help to recognize it yourself.

Name it

Saying "I am grieving" out loud, or writing it down, can affirm what you are going through.

Remember what the relationship meant

Your grief reflects the meaning of the bond, not how others rank it. Writing about the relationship can help you honor it.

Notice the secondary losses

Disenfranchised losses often come with secondary losses that also deserve grief: lost futures, lost roles, lost hopes.

Creating your own rituals

If there was no funeral, or you could not attend, you can create your own way to mourn.

  • Write a letter to the person or to what you lost
  • Plant something in their memory
  • Visit a meaningful place
  • Hold a small gathering with people who understand
  • Light a candle on significant dates
  • Make a memory book or box

See Grief Rituals for more ideas.

Finding people who understand

Support can be especially important when grief is disenfranchised. You might find it in:

  • Support groups for specific losses, such as pet loss, pregnancy loss, or suicide loss
  • Online communities of people with similar experiences
  • A counselor who understands disenfranchised grief
  • One friend who is willing to listen without judgment

You do not need everyone to understand. Sometimes one person is enough.

Talking to others about your grief

If you want others to acknowledge your loss, you might try:

  • "This loss is really hard for me. I would appreciate it if you could just check in sometimes."
  • "I know it might not seem like a big loss, but it means a lot to me."
  • "I do not need advice. I just need you to know I am grieving."

You are not obligated to explain or justify your grief. But sometimes a simple sentence can open space for support.

If you are supporting someone

If someone you know is grieving a loss others might overlook:

  • Acknowledge it directly: "I am so sorry about your loss."
  • Ask about the person or what they lost
  • Do not compare or minimize
  • Remember significant dates
  • Offer the same care you would for any other loss

See Supporting a Grieving Loved One.

When to seek more help

Disenfranchised grief can be heavy, and carrying it alone is hard. Consider reaching out to a grief counselor if your grief feels stuck, overwhelming, or isolating for a long time.

If you are having thoughts of ending your life, please call or text 988 to reach the Suicide and Crisis Lifeline. You do not have to be in immediate danger to call.

Grief at work when no one knows

Disenfranchised losses rarely come with bereavement leave. You may be expected at work the day after your dog dies, or the week after a miscarriage no one knew about. If you can, consider using sick time or a personal day, and know that you do not have to say why. If a manager is supportive, a short honest sentence like "I have had a significant loss and need a day" can be enough. Taking time to grieve is not an overreaction. It is care.

Giving your grief a witness

One of the hardest parts of unrecognized grief is that no one sees it. A witness can change that. A witness is simply one person who knows what you lost and treats it as real. It might be a friend, a counselor, someone in a support group, or even a person who shared the same kind of loss.

You can also be your own witness. Write down who or what you lost, what they meant to you, and what you miss most. Read it out loud. Light a candle or set out a photo. These small acts say that this loss happened and it mattered.

If you would like others to understand, you might try a single plain sentence: "I am grieving, and it would help if you asked me about them." Many people respond with more care than you expect once they are given permission.

Your loss is real

Whatever you lost, and however others see it, your grief is real. It reflects something that mattered to you. You deserve room to mourn, people to support you, and ways to honor what you lost. Even if the world does not recognize your grief, you can.
