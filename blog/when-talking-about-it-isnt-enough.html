Coping & Healing

Why Being Told to "Talk About It" Isn't Always Enough

The Murido TeamOct 2, 2026 · 5 min read

"You should talk about it." It is one of the most common pieces of advice grieving people hear. And for many, talking does help. Sharing memories, saying how you feel, and being heard can bring real relief.

But some people find that talking about their grief does not seem to help much. They talk and talk, and nothing shifts. Others find that talking makes them feel worse, or that they simply do not have the words. If that sounds like you, this guide is for you. It explores why talking is not always enough, and what else can help grief move.

Why talking can help

Before exploring the limits of talking, it is worth acknowledging what talking does well.

  • It reduces isolation. Sharing grief with someone can make you feel less alone.
  • It makes the loss real. Saying what happened out loud can help your mind absorb it.
  • It honors the person. Telling stories keeps their memory alive.
  • It brings validation. Being heard can reassure you that your feelings make sense.

For many people, these benefits are powerful. Talking is often an important part of grieving.

Why talking is not always enough

Talking has limits. Here are some reasons it may not feel like enough for you.

Grief lives in the body

Grief is not only a set of thoughts. It also shows up in the body as tight chests, heavy limbs, exhaustion, and restlessness. Talking may not reach those physical sensations. Our guide to the physical side of grief explains this connection.

Words can keep feelings at a distance

Sometimes talking about grief becomes a way of thinking about feelings rather than feeling them. You might describe your grief in detail without actually letting yourself feel it. This can be protective, but it may not help grief move.

Talking can turn into rumination

Some people find themselves telling the same story over and over, especially about the death or what they could have done differently. This can become a loop that deepens pain rather than easing it. Our guide to rumination in grief explains why.

Not everyone processes through words

Some people are naturally more inclined to process through action, movement, creativity, or quiet reflection. For them, talking may not be the most natural path.

The listener matters

If the people you talk to respond with clichés, try to fix things, or change the subject, talking can leave you feeling more alone.

Being told to talk about it assumes grief lives only in words. Often, it lives in your hands, your chest, and your days.

Other ways grief can move

If talking feels limited, there are many other ways to work with grief.

Moving your body

Physical movement can help release tension that grief stores in the body. This might include:

  • Walking, especially outdoors.
  • Gentle stretching or yoga.
  • Dancing to music they loved.
  • Gardening or physical tasks.

Movement does not have to be intense. Even a slow walk can help shift something.

Creating something

Creative expression can give grief a shape when words fall short. You might try:

  • Drawing or painting.
  • Making a collage of photos.
  • Knitting, sewing, or woodworking.
  • Writing music or poetry.
  • Cooking their favorite recipes.

The goal is not to create something beautiful. It is to give your feelings somewhere to go.

Writing instead of speaking

Writing can be different from talking. It is private, slower, and allows space for feelings that might be hard to say aloud. Some ideas:

  1. Write a letter to the person who died.
  2. Write about a specific memory in detail.
  3. Write what you wish you could say.
  4. Write a list of things you miss.
  5. Write what you would tell someone else going through this.

Rituals

Rituals turn feelings into actions. Lighting a candle, visiting a grave, or holding a small ceremony can bring a sense of meaning and connection. Our guide to grief rituals offers many ideas.

Mindfulness

Mindfulness practices invite you to feel grief directly, in your body and in the present moment, rather than talking about it. This can be a powerful way to let grief move. Our guide to mindful grieving has simple practices.

Being with nature

Many grieving people find comfort in nature. Sitting by water, walking in the woods, or watching a sunset can bring a quiet sense of connection that words cannot capture.

Acting on values

Sometimes what helps is not talking about grief but living in a way that honors the person. Volunteering for a cause they cared about, continuing a tradition, or doing something they would have loved can carry their influence forward.

When talking makes things worse

For some people, talking about grief consistently leaves them feeling more distressed. This can happen if:

  • The death was traumatic, and retelling it brings up intense images.
  • You feel pressure to talk before you are ready.
  • The people listening are not supportive.
  • Talking pulls you into loops of guilt or regret.

If this sounds familiar, it is okay to take a break from talking. You might focus on other ways of grieving for a while. If the death was traumatic, working with a therapist who specializes in trauma can help you approach the memories in a safer, more structured way. Our guide to intrusive memories after a death may also help.

Finding the right kind of support

If you have tried talking and it has not helped, it may be worth looking for support that works differently. For example:

  • Grief counseling that includes body based, creative, or acceptance based approaches.
  • Support groups that include activities, not just discussion.
  • Art or music therapy, which uses creative expression.
  • Movement based classes, like gentle yoga designed for grief.

Our guide to what actually helps with grief explores a range of approaches.

Mixing approaches

Talking does not have to be all or nothing. Many people find that a mix of approaches works best. You might talk with a friend once a week, take daily walks, write letters on hard days, and light a candle on anniversaries.

Pay attention to what helps. If something brings a sense of relief or connection, even small, it is worth keeping. If something leaves you feeling worse each time, it may be worth trying something else.

Grief beyond words

Talking is one way to grieve, but it is not the only way. Grief can move through your body, your hands, your rituals, and your daily choices. If words feel like they are not enough, you are not doing grief wrong. You may simply need other paths.

Your grief is yours. You are allowed to find the ways that help you carry it, whether they involve talking, walking, writing, creating, or simply sitting in quiet remembrance.
