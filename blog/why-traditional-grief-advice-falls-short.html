Coping & Healing

Why Traditional Grief Advice Falls Short

The Murido TeamOct 17, 2026 · 6 min read

"Stay busy." "Be strong for the kids." "Time heals all wounds." "Do not dwell on it." "You need closure." "They would not want you to be sad."

If you have grieved, you have probably heard some version of this advice. It usually comes from people who care about you and want to help. And sometimes it does help a little. But many grieving people find that traditional advice leaves them feeling more alone, more confused, or more ashamed of their grief.

This guide looks at some of the most common pieces of grief advice, why they often fall short, and what a gentler, more realistic approach might look like.

Why traditional advice exists

Most grief advice comes from a good place. People want to ease your pain. They want to see you feel better. They may also feel uncomfortable with sadness, both yours and their own, and want to help it pass quickly.

Many common phrases are also shaped by cultural messages about grief: that it should be private, short, and tidy. That strong people do not cry. That moving on is the goal.

These messages can make it hard for grieving people to feel that their real experience is acceptable.

Most grief advice tries to make the pain go away. What grieving people often need is permission to feel it.

Common advice, and why it falls short

"Stay busy"

Staying active can provide welcome breaks from grief. But when busyness becomes the main strategy, it can become a way of never feeling anything. Grief that is constantly pushed aside may build up and arrive later, often at inconvenient times.

A gentler alternative: Balance activity with time to grieve. Breaks are healthy. So is making space to feel.

"Be strong"

This often means hiding tears, holding it together, and not burdening others. But suppressing grief can be exhausting. It can also teach others, especially children, that grief is something to hide.

A gentler alternative: Strength can include crying, asking for help, and being honest about how you feel.

"Time heals all wounds"

Time does often soften grief. But time alone is not what helps. What you do with that time, and the support you receive, matters. Some people find that grief becomes harder before it becomes easier. Our guide to why grief can feel worse in year two explains why.

A gentler alternative: Time can help, especially when paired with support, connection, and space to grieve.

"Do not dwell on it"

This advice suggests that thinking about the person or the loss is harmful. But avoiding thoughts and memories often makes them more intrusive. Remembering can be an important part of grieving.

A gentler alternative: Let memories come. If you notice yourself caught in painful loops, that is a different issue. Our guide to rumination in grief explains the difference.

"You need closure"

Closure implies that grief can be neatly finished. For many people, grief never fully closes. They continue to love and miss the person throughout their lives.

A gentler alternative: Instead of closure, many people find a way to carry the loss. Our guide to continuing bonds explores staying connected.

"They would not want you to be sad"

This may be true, but it can make you feel guilty for grieving. Sadness is a natural response to losing someone you love.

A gentler alternative: They would probably want you to be okay eventually. They would also likely understand that you miss them.

"Everything happens for a reason"

For some, this brings comfort. For many, it feels dismissive or even hurtful, especially after a sudden or tragic death.

A gentler alternative: "I do not understand why this happened. I am so sorry."

The problem with stages and timelines

Many people grow up hearing that grief follows a set of stages or should be "done" within a certain time. When their grief does not match, they may worry that something is wrong.

In reality, grief is much more varied. It can loop back, include many feelings at once, and last much longer than people expect. Our guide to beyond the five stages explores why the stages often do not fit.

What grieving people often need instead

When grieving people describe what actually helped, they often mention:

  1. Being listened to, without being fixed.
  2. Hearing their loved one's name.
  3. Not being rushed.
  4. Practical help, especially after the first few weeks.
  5. Permission to feel whatever they feel.
  6. Support that continues over time.

Our guide to supporting a grieving loved one shares many more ideas.

Newer ways of thinking about grief

Over time, counselors and grief researchers have developed approaches that move beyond traditional advice. Some of these include:

  • Allowing feelings rather than fighting them. Acceptance based approaches suggest that making room for grief helps it move.
  • Moving between grief and life. Healthy grieving often involves going back and forth between focusing on the loss and focusing on daily life.
  • Continuing bonds. Staying connected to the person who died can be healthy.
  • Meaning making. Finding ways to make sense of the loss and carry the person's influence forward.
  • Self compassion. Treating yourself with kindness in grief.

Our guide to mindful grieving offers a taste of this gentler approach.

When you are given unhelpful advice

You may continue to hear traditional advice from people around you. You can choose how to respond. Some options:

  • Let it go. Recognize that they mean well, and do not take it to heart.
  • Gently redirect. "I know you want me to feel better. Right now I just need someone to listen."
  • Set a boundary. "I would rather not hear that. It does not help me."
  • Share what helps. "What would really help is if you checked in next month."

You do not have to accept advice that does not fit you.

Being your own guide

At its heart, grief is personal. No advice, however wise, can tell you exactly how you should feel or what you should do. You are the expert on your own grief.

It can help to ask yourself:

  • What do I need today?
  • What helps me feel even a little more connected or grounded?
  • What advice have I been given that does not fit me?
  • What would I say to a friend going through this?

Rewriting advice in your own words

One helpful exercise is to take a piece of advice you have heard and rewrite it so it fits you. "Stay strong" might become "Let me be strong enough to cry." "Time heals all wounds" might become "Time, kindness, and company will help me carry this." "Move on" might become "Move forward, and bring them with me."

Writing your own versions can turn advice that felt dismissive into something that actually supports you. You can keep these phrases somewhere you will see them, and return to them when other people's words do not fit.

A different kind of advice

If there is any advice that tends to hold up, it may be this: be gentle with yourself. Let grief be what it is. Lean on people who can sit with you in it. Take care of your body. And remember that grief reflects love.

Traditional advice often tries to make grief smaller. A gentler approach makes room for grief to be as big as it needs to be, while helping you slowly build a life that holds both your loss and your love.
