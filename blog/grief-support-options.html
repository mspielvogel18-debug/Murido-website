Grief Support Options: Counseling, Groups, Medication, and More

The Murido TeamOct 8, 2026 · 5 min read

When you are grieving, you may know you need support but not know where to start. There are many options: counseling, support groups, medication, faith communities, online resources, apps, and the people already in your life. Each offers something different, and what works best depends on your loss, your needs, and your circumstances.

This guide walks through the main kinds of grief support, what each one offers, and how to decide what might fit you. You do not have to choose just one. Many people find a combination works best.

Start with what you need

Before looking at specific options, it can help to ask yourself a few questions:

  1. Do I need someone to talk to? A friend, counselor, or group may help.
  2. Do I need people who understand my specific loss? A specialized support group may help.
  3. Am I struggling to function? Professional support may be important.
  4. Do I need practical help? Friends, family, or community organizations may help.
  5. Do I need tools for hard moments? Books, apps, or journaling may help.
  6. Am I feeling hopeless or unsafe? Immediate professional or crisis support is important.

Your answers can guide your choices. They may also change over time.

The best support is the support that meets you where you are right now.

Friends and family

The people already in your life are often the first source of support. They knew you, and sometimes the person who died. They can offer companionship, practical help, and shared memories.

What they offer

  • Familiarity and comfort.
  • Help with daily tasks.
  • Shared grief, if they also knew the person.
  • Ongoing presence over time.

Limits

Friends and family may be grieving too, or may not know how to help. Some may say unhelpful things or drift away after the funeral. That is not a failure on your part.

If you want to help the people around you support you better, you might share our guide to supporting a grieving loved one.

Grief counseling and therapy

Counseling with a trained professional offers a dedicated space to explore your grief. Counselors can help you understand your feelings, develop coping strategies, and work through complicated aspects of loss.

Who it may help most

  • People whose grief feels stuck or overwhelming.
  • People who experienced a traumatic death.
  • People struggling with guilt, anger, or intrusive memories.
  • People whose grief overlaps with depression or anxiety.
  • People who have little support elsewhere.

What to know

Counselors use different approaches. It is fine to ask how they work and to switch if the fit is not right. Our guide to grief counseling explains what to expect.

Support groups

Support groups bring together people who have experienced loss. They may be general or focused on specific losses, such as the death of a spouse, child, or parent, or loss to suicide or overdose.

What they offer

  • Connection with people who understand.
  • A sense of not being alone.
  • Practical tips from others' experiences.
  • A regular, structured space for grief.

Types of groups

  • Facilitated groups, led by a trained professional.
  • Peer led groups, led by people who have experienced loss.
  • Drop in groups, which you can attend as needed.
  • Closed groups, which meet for a set number of weeks with the same members.
  • Online groups, which meet by video or through forums.

Groups are not for everyone. Some people feel uncomfortable sharing in a group setting. It may help to try a group a few times before deciding.

Medication

Grief itself is not an illness, and there is no medication that treats grief. However, if grief is accompanied by depression, severe anxiety, or significant sleep problems, medication may help some people.

What to know

  • Medication decisions should be made with a doctor.
  • Medication may ease certain symptoms, making it easier to engage with grief.
  • Medication is often most helpful combined with other support, like counseling.

Our guide to grief alongside depression and anxiety explains how these can overlap and when to seek help.

Faith communities

Many people find support through their faith communities. Clergy, chaplains, and members of congregations may offer comfort, prayer, ritual, and companionship.

What they offer

  • Spiritual comfort and meaning.
  • Rituals for remembrance.
  • A built in community.
  • Ongoing care, sometimes for years.

If grief has shaken your faith, this kind of support may feel complicated. Our guide to when grief shakes your faith may help.

Hospice bereavement programs

If your loved one was in hospice care, the hospice may offer bereavement support to the family. This can include counseling, support groups, and check ins. Some hospices offer services to the wider community as well.

Online resources and apps

Online resources have grown a great deal. These include:

  • Educational articles, like the guides on this site.
  • Online forums where grieving people share experiences.
  • Online therapy platforms.
  • Grief apps, which may offer journaling, guided reflections, memory keeping, or conversation tools.

What they offer

  • Access at any time, including late at night.
  • Privacy and flexibility.
  • Connection with people far away.
  • Tools for reflection between other forms of support.

Limits

Online resources are not a substitute for professional help when grief is severe or when someone is at risk. If you use apps, look at how they handle your privacy. Our guide to the real cost of grief support covers questions to ask.

Books and creative resources

Books about grief, memoirs, poetry, and podcasts can help you feel understood and offer new perspectives. Creative outlets like art, music, and writing can also provide powerful ways to process grief.

Self guided practices

Some support you can give yourself:

  • Journaling or letter writing.
  • Mindfulness and breathing practices.
  • Rituals of remembrance.
  • Gentle movement and time outdoors.
  • Self compassion practices.

Our guides to mindful grieving and grief rituals offer ideas.

Combining options

Many people use several forms of support at once or at different times. For example:

  • Seeing a counselor every other week.
  • Attending a monthly support group.
  • Journaling on hard days.
  • Leaning on a close friend for weekly walks.
  • Using a reflection app between sessions.

You can adjust as your needs change. Support that helped in the first months may not be what you need in year two, and that is okay.

When to seek urgent help

If you are having thoughts of suicide or self harm, feel unable to keep yourself safe, or are unable to care for yourself or dependents, please seek help right away. In the United States, you can call or text 988 at any time. You can also go to an emergency room or call emergency services.

Finding your mix

There is no single right way to get support for grief. What matters is finding something that helps you feel less alone and more able to carry your loss. You can start small, with one call, one group, or one conversation, and build from there.

You deserve support, whatever your loss, however long it has been. Reaching out is not a sign of weakness. It is a sign that you are taking your grief, and yourself, seriously.
