date: 2026-09-02
---
Grief counseling is support from a trained professional who specializes in loss. Many people wonder whether they need it, what happens in sessions, and how to find someone. Here is a plain guide.

## Who it is for

You do not have to be in crisis to see a grief counselor. People seek counseling to make sense of a loss, cope with guilt, manage a difficult family situation, or simply have a dedicated space to talk. It can be especially helpful after sudden, traumatic, or multiple losses.

## What happens in sessions

Sessions usually involve talking about the person, the death, and how grief is showing up in your life. A counselor may help you explore feelings, notice patterns, and find ways to cope. Some use specific approaches, like writing exercises, imaginal conversations, or planning for anniversaries.

## Counseling vs. therapy

Grief counselors focus specifically on loss. Therapists may address grief alongside other issues. For prolonged grief, specialized therapies have strong research support, so ask whether a provider is trained in them.

## Finding a counselor

- Ask your doctor for a referral
- Check hospice organizations, which often offer bereavement support
- Search directories for grief or bereavement specialists
- Ask about sliding-scale fees if cost is a concern

## What to look for

A good fit matters more than credentials alone. You should feel heard, not rushed, and respected in how you grieve. It is okay to try a few counselors before choosing one.

## Using other support alongside

Many people use counseling alongside groups, books, or apps like Murido for the hours between sessions. If you are ever in crisis, call or text 988 rather than waiting for your next appointment.

title: What Actually Helps With Grief: Approaches That Work
desc: From continuing bonds to support groups to specialized therapy, here are grief approaches with real support behind them.
date: 2026-09-05
---
There is no single cure for grief, but there are approaches that consistently help people carry it. Here is what research and the experience of grieving people suggest.

## Social support

Having people who listen, say the name, and stay present over time is one of the strongest protections in grief. Quality matters more than quantity. One or two understanding people can make a big difference.

## Expressing grief

Talking, writing, crying, art, and ritual all give grief somewhere to go. Expressive writing about loss has been studied for decades and many people find it helpful.

## Continuing bonds

Staying connected to the person through memories, rituals, and inner conversations is now widely seen as a healthy part of grief for many people.

## Balancing loss and life

The dual process model suggests people do best when they move between facing grief and engaging with daily life. Both sides matter.

## Caring for the body

Sleep, food, and movement affect mood and resilience. Small steps count.

## Support groups

Groups let people share with others who understand. Loss-specific groups, such as for parents, partners, or suicide loss, can be especially powerful.

## Specialized therapy

For prolonged grief, therapies designed for it have good evidence. For trauma after a death, trauma-focused therapy helps.

## What usually does not help

Suppressing grief for long periods, isolating, relying on alcohol or drugs, and holding yourself to a deadline tend to make things harder.

## Finding your mix

Most people use a combination. Experiment and keep what helps. If nothing seems to help and you feel unsafe, call or text 988.

title: Facing the Hard Places: Returning to Spaces That Remind You of Them
desc: Their favorite restaurant, the hospital, their room. How to approach places filled with memories at your own pace.
date: 2026-09-08
---
Some places hold so much of a person that walking into them feels impossible: their bedroom, their favorite cafe, the beach where you scattered ashes, the hospital where they died. Avoiding these places is natural. Over time, though, avoidance can shrink your world.

## Why avoidance makes sense

Places carry memories, and memories carry pain. Avoiding them protects you in the short term, especially in early grief.

## When avoidance becomes limiting

If you skip entire neighborhoods, events, or family homes for years, avoidance may be keeping grief stuck. It can also mean missing places that could bring comfort.

## A gradual approach

- Start with a place that feels a little hard, not the hardest
- Bring someone with you if that helps
- Go for a short time and leave when you need to
- Notice what you feel and let it come
- Repeat, staying a bit longer each time

## Making it meaningful

Some people bring a photo, order the person's favorite meal, or say a few words when they arrive. Turning a hard place into a place of remembrance can change how it feels.

## Some places you may never want to revisit

You do not have to return everywhere. If a place connects only to trauma, it is reasonable to let it go. The goal is a life that is not limited by fear, not forcing yourself into pain.

## Support along the way

A grief counselor can help plan a gradual approach. If visiting a place brings up overwhelming distress or thoughts of harm, call or text 988.

title: Breaking the Avoidance Cycle in Grief
desc: Avoiding reminders of loss brings relief that does not last. Learn how the avoidance cycle works and how to gently step out of it.
date: 2026-09-11
---
You avoid their photos, their name, their room, their music. Each time, there is relief. But over time, the list of things you cannot face grows. This is the avoidance cycle, and it can keep grief locked in place.

## How the cycle works

A reminder brings pain. You avoid it. The pain drops, which teaches your brain that avoidance works. Next time, you avoid sooner. Meanwhile, the grief connected to that reminder does not get processed.

## Signs you might be caught in it

- Changing the subject whenever they come up
- Keeping their belongings boxed and untouched for years
- Skipping family events or holidays
- Staying constantly busy so there is no quiet time
- Feeling panic at unexpected reminders

## Stepping out gently

- Pick one small reminder to approach, like a single photo
- Spend a few minutes with it and let feelings come
- Notice that the feeling rises and then falls
- Build up gradually to bigger reminders

## Balance matters

Not all avoidance is bad. Taking breaks from grief is healthy. The question is whether avoidance is a rest stop or a wall.

## Using remembrance as a bridge

Memory prompts, letters, and shared stories offer structured, manageable ways to approach grief. They let you turn toward the person in small doses, on your terms.

## If avoidance comes from trauma

If reminders trigger flashbacks or panic, trauma may be involved. A trauma-informed therapist can help. If distress becomes unsafe, call or text 988.

desc: Rituals give grief a shape and a time. Ideas for meaningful grief rituals, from daily practices to annual traditions.
date: 2026-09-14
---
Rituals have helped humans grieve for as long as there have been humans. Funerals are one kind, but personal rituals, small, repeated acts of remembrance, can be just as powerful in the months and years afterward.

## Why rituals help

Rituals give grief a place and time. Rather than grief ambushing you all day, a ritual offers a dedicated moment to remember. They also create continuity, a way to keep doing something with or for the person.

## Daily or weekly rituals

- Lighting a candle each evening
- Saying good morning to their photo
- Drinking coffee from their mug
- Writing one memory a day
- Visiting their grave or a meaningful place weekly

## Annual rituals

- Cooking their favorite meal on their birthday
- Gathering family to share stories on the anniversary
- Doing an activity they loved, like a hike or a game
- Donating to a cause they cared about
- Writing them a yearly letter

## Rituals for hard moments

Some people create rituals for when grief hits hard: playing a specific song, holding an object, or stepping outside to talk to them.

## Rituals can change

What feels right in the first year may change later. Let rituals evolve as your grief does.

## Shared rituals

Inviting others to join creates connection. A shared memory book where family adds a story each year is one way to build a ritual together. If grief becomes too heavy, call or text 988.

title: Making Decisions After a Loss: Belongings, Homes, and Hard Choices
desc: What to do with their things, whether to move, how to handle money. A gentle guide to making decisions while grieving.
date: 2026-09-17
---
After a death, decisions pile up: what to do with clothes, whether to sell the house, how to handle the estate. Grief makes decisions harder, and many people feel paralyzed or rush into choices they later regret.

## Why decisions feel so hard

Grief brain affects concentration and memory. Every choice can feel loaded, as if keeping or giving away a sweater is a statement about how much you loved them. And there is no certainty about what you will want later.

## Separate urgent from non-urgent

Some decisions must be made quickly, such as funeral arrangements and certain legal or financial tasks. Many others can wait. A common piece of advice is to delay major non-urgent decisions, like moving, for a while if you can.

## Belongings

- There is no deadline to sort through their things
- Start with less emotional items
- Keep a few meaningful objects close
- Photograph items before letting them go
- Invite others to choose keepsakes

## Living with uncertainty

You may never feel completely sure. Instead of waiting for certainty, ask which choice best fits your values and needs right now. Accept that you can only decide with what you know today.

## Get help

Trusted friends, family, financial advisors, and attorneys can help. Write decisions down so you do not have to hold them in your head.

## Be gentle with past choices

If you later regret a decision, remember you made it while grieving. Treat yourself kindly. If decisions feel overwhelming to the point of hopelessness, call or text 988.

desc: The first holiday, the first trip, the first grocery run. Why grief firsts are so hard and how to get through them.
date: 2026-09-20
---
The first time you go to their favorite restaurant without them. The first birthday. The first time you hear good news and cannot call them. Grief firsts can feel impossible, and they come constantly in the first year.

## Why firsts hurt so much

Firsts make the absence concrete. Each one confirms the new reality in a specific, unavoidable way. Your brain is learning that they are not there for this moment either.

## Anticipation is often worse

Many people find the days before a first are harder than the day itself. Dread builds. Knowing this can help you plan for those lead-up days too.

## Planning for firsts

- Decide in advance how you want to spend the day
- Include a way to honor them
- Have someone you can call
- Build in an exit plan for gatherings
- Allow for changing your plan at the last minute

## Small firsts count too

Not all firsts are holidays. First time cooking for one. First time filling out a form that asks for marital status. These can blindside you. Be kind to yourself when they do.

## Doing it anyway

Firsts are hard, but getting through them often brings a quiet sense of strength. You did not want to learn that you could survive this, and you did.

## After the firsts

Seconds can be hard too, as covered in our year two guide. Keep the same gentleness. If any first brings thoughts of not wanting to go on, call or text 988.

Grief support is valuable, but cost can be a real barrier. Here is an honest overview of common options and how to find support within your means. Costs vary widely by location and provider, so always check directly.

## Free options

- Hospice bereavement programs, often free to families of patients and sometimes to the community
- Peer support groups through community centers, faith communities, and nonprofits
- Online communities and forums
- Books from the library

## Low-cost options

- Sliding-scale counseling based on income
- Community mental health centers
- University training clinics with supervised trainees
- Employee assistance programs through work, which often include a few free sessions

## Private counseling

Private grief counseling or therapy can be expensive. Insurance may cover some sessions if the provider is in network, especially if grief is accompanied by depression or anxiety. Check your plan.

## Apps and digital tools

Grief apps range from free to subscription. Murido, for example, is free to download, with Murido Pro at $59.99 per year for its full features. Apps are not a replacement for counseling, but they can offer support between sessions or when other options are out of reach.

## Choosing what fits

Consider what you most need: someone to talk to, practical tools, community, or clinical care. Many people combine a free group with occasional counseling or a digital tool.

## Crisis support is free

Crisis support should never depend on money. In the US, 988 is free and available at any hour by call or text.

title: Checking Their Phone, Keeping Their Voicemail: Grief Habits and What They Mean
desc: Calling their number, sleeping with their shirt, keeping their room untouched. When grief habits help and when they may hold you back.
date: 2026-09-26
---
You call their voicemail just to hear their voice. You sleep with their shirt. You keep their room exactly as it was. Many grieving people have habits like these and wonder whether they are healthy.

## Most of these habits are normal

Keeping connection to someone through objects, voices, and routines is a very human response to loss. These habits are often forms of continuing bonds, and many people find them comforting.

## When habits help

- They bring comfort or a sense of closeness
- You can choose when to do them
- They coexist with engaging in daily life
- They feel like connection rather than compulsion

## When habits may be holding you back

- You feel intense anxiety if you cannot do them
- They take up large amounts of time
- They prevent you from engaging with others or your own life
- They feed a belief that the person is not really gone

## Adjusting gently

If a habit feels stuck, you do not have to stop abruptly. You might reduce frequency, change the form, or pair it with something new. For example, instead of calling their voicemail daily, you might save it and listen on meaningful days, or write them a message instead.

## Turning habits into rituals

Many grief habits can become intentional rituals, chosen and meaningful rather than driven by panic. That shift often makes them more comforting.

## Talk it through

If you are unsure whether a habit is helping, a grief counselor can help you explore it. If habits are tied to feeling you cannot live without them, call or text 988.

title: When Grief Feels Overwhelming: Ways Through the Storm
desc: Practical, in-the-moment techniques for when grief feels like too much, plus when to reach out for urgent help.
date: 2026-09-29
---
There are moments in grief when it feels like you cannot breathe, cannot think, cannot survive the next minute. If you are in one of those moments, this is for you.

## First, safety

If you are thinking about harming yourself, call or text 988 now, or call 911 if you are in immediate danger. You do not need to be sure it is serious. Reaching out is enough reason.

## Slow your body

- Breathe in for four counts, out for six. Repeat several times.
- Put your feet flat on the floor and press down.
- Hold something cold, like ice or a cold drink.
- Name five things you can see and four you can hear.

## Let the wave move

Intense grief often peaks and then eases. Reminding yourself, "This is a wave, and waves pass," can help you ride it rather than fight it.

## Reach out

Text a friend: "Having a really hard moment. Can you talk?" You do not need to explain. Many people want to be called in these moments. If no one is available, a companion app or crisis line can help bridge the gap.

## After the storm

When it passes, do something gentle: drink water, eat something small, rest. Notice what triggered the wave, if anything, so you can plan for next time.

## When storms are frequent

If overwhelming moments are happening often, talk to a doctor or grief counselor. Frequent intensity can be a sign you need more support, and that support is available.
