title: Why Being Told to "Talk About It" Isn't Always Enough
desc: Talking helps many grieving people, but not everyone, and not always. Other ways to process grief when words fall short.
date: 2026-10-02
---
Talk about it. It is one of the most common pieces of grief advice. Talking can help a great deal, but some people find it is not enough, or not their style at all.

## Why talking helps

Putting feelings into words can make them more manageable. Being heard by someone who cares eases isolation. Telling the story of the loss can help the mind integrate it.

## Why it might not be enough

- Some people process internally rather than out loud
- Talking without direction can turn into repeating the same painful loop
- Others may not know how to listen without fixing or minimizing
- Some feelings are hard to put into words at all

## Grieving styles differ

Researchers describe a range of grieving styles. Some people grieve intuitively, through emotion and talking. Others grieve instrumentally, through action and thinking: building a memorial, finishing a project, organizing. Many people blend both. Neither style is better.

## Other ways to process

- Writing letters, journals, or memories
- Creating something: art, music, a garden, a quilt
- Physical activity like walking, running, or woodworking
- Rituals and ceremonies
- Guided reflection with specific prompts

## Structure can help

Open-ended talking can feel aimless. Structured prompts, like "What is one thing you want them to know?" or "Describe their hands," can make reflection more focused and meaningful.

## Talk to the right people

Talking is most helpful with listeners who can sit with pain. Grief groups and counselors are trained for this. If you are carrying something too heavy to hold alone, call or text 988.

title: Living With Unanswered Questions After a Death
desc: Why did it happen? Did they suffer? Did they know I loved them? How to live with the questions grief cannot answer.
date: 2026-10-05
---
Many deaths leave questions that will never be fully answered. Why did it happen? Could it have been prevented? Were they afraid? Did they know how much I loved them? Living with uncertainty is one of the hardest parts of grief.

## Why the mind needs answers

Humans crave certainty, especially about things that matter most. Unanswered questions keep the mind searching, which can lead to rumination and distress.

## Seek what answers you can

Sometimes partial answers are available. Talking to doctors, reading reports, or speaking with people who were there can help. It is okay to seek information, as long as it is helping rather than deepening a loop.

## Accepting what cannot be known

At some point, many questions simply have no answer. Acceptance does not mean you stop caring about them. It means you allow them to stay open while you continue living.

## Holding uncertainty with compassion

- Acknowledge the question: "I may never know if she was scared."
- Notice the feeling under it, often love or fear
- Respond to the feeling: "I wanted her to be safe."
- Return to what you do know: that you loved her, and that she mattered

## The question "Did they know?"

This one is especially common. Most relationships are built on thousands of moments of care, not on final words. Writing a letter that says what you wish you had said can bring some peace.

## When uncertainty becomes unbearable

If questions are consuming your days, a grief counselor can help you find ways to hold them. If you feel unsafe, call or text 988.

desc: An overview of grief support options, what each offers, and how to choose what is right for you.
date: 2026-10-08
---
If you are looking for grief support, the options can feel overwhelming. Here is an overview of common choices and what each one offers.

## Peer support groups

Groups bring together people who have experienced similar losses. They offer understanding, practical tips, and the relief of not having to explain yourself. Many are free.

## Grief counseling

One-on-one support from a professional who specializes in loss. Useful for working through complicated feelings, guilt, family conflict, or sudden or traumatic loss.

## Therapy for prolonged grief or trauma

If grief remains intense and impairing for a long time, or a death was traumatic, specialized therapies can help.

## Medication

Grief on its own is not usually treated with medication. But if grief triggers depression, anxiety, or sleep problems, a doctor may suggest medication as part of care. This is a conversation to have with a professional.

## Spiritual support

Chaplains, clergy, and spiritual communities offer support rooted in faith and meaning.

## Self-help tools

Books, journals, workshops, and apps can support grief between other forms of care. Murido is one such tool, offering companions, memory prompts, and letters, but it is not therapy or crisis support.

## Choosing what fits

Ask yourself: Do I need to be understood, guided, treated, or connected? You may need different things at different times. It is fine to try several.

## Crisis support

In a crisis, do not wait for an appointment. Call or text 988 at any hour.

title: Supporting a Grieving Loved One: A Guide for Family and Friends
desc: What to say, what not to say, and how to help someone who is grieving over the long haul.
date: 2026-10-11
---
When someone you love is grieving, you may desperately want to help and have no idea how. This guide offers practical ways to show up.

## Say something

Silence often hurts more than imperfect words. Try: "I am so sorry. I am thinking of you." Or: "I don't know what to say, but I'm here."

## Say their name

Many grieving people long to hear the person's name. Share a memory. Ask about them. It shows the person is not forgotten.

## Avoid common pitfalls

- "Everything happens for a reason"
- "They are in a better place"
- "At least..."
- "I know exactly how you feel"
- Pushing them to move on

These can feel dismissive even when meant kindly.

## Offer specific help

Instead of "Let me know if you need anything," try "Can I bring dinner Tuesday?" or "I'm going to the store. What can I pick up?"

## Show up over time

Support often fades after a few weeks. Put reminders in your calendar for months later and for birthdays and anniversaries. A simple text on a hard date can mean a great deal.

## Listen more than you talk

You do not need to fix anything. Listening, being present, and allowing tears is often the most helpful thing.

## Watch for warning signs

If your loved one talks about not wanting to live, cannot function for a long period, or relies heavily on alcohol or drugs, encourage professional help. If they are in danger, help them contact 988 or emergency services.

title: The Honest Truth About Healing From Grief
desc: Healing from grief does not look like getting over it. An honest picture of what healing actually looks like.
date: 2026-10-14
---
When people talk about healing from grief, they often picture a finish line: the day you feel normal again. The truth is more complicated, and in some ways more hopeful.

## Healing is not forgetting

Healing does not mean you stop missing them or that the loss stops mattering. It means grief no longer controls your life.

## It is not linear

Healing involves setbacks, hard days, and unexpected waves. Progress often looks like a jagged line that trends upward over months and years.

## What healing can look like

- You can think about them with love, not only pain
- Hard days still come, but you trust they will pass
- You have rebuilt routines and relationships
- You feel a sense of meaning or purpose again
- You carry them with you in ways that feel good

## Healing includes change

Many people say loss changed them permanently. Some describe becoming more compassionate, clearer about what matters, or more present. This is sometimes called post-traumatic growth, though it never makes the loss worth it.

## There is no deadline

Some people find their footing within a year or two. Others take longer. Comparing your timeline to anyone else's is rarely helpful.

## Support speeds nothing but helps everything

Support may not make grief shorter, but it makes it more bearable. Lean on people, tools, and professionals. If you ever feel unsafe, call or text 988.

cat: coping-and-healing
title: Why Traditional Grief Advice Falls Short
desc: Let go, stay busy, get closure. Why some traditional grief advice misses the mark and what to try instead.
date: 2026-10-17
---
Much of the grief advice people hear has been passed down for generations. Some of it is wise. Some of it, according to what grieving people and researchers report, can make things harder.

## "Let go"

The detachment model of grief once advised cutting ties with the person who died. Research on continuing bonds suggests many people do better by staying connected in healthy ways.

## "Stay busy"

Distraction is useful in moderation, but constant busyness can postpone grief rather than help process it.

## "Get closure"

Closure suggests grief can be wrapped up. Many people never feel fully closed, and that is okay. Integration, carrying the loss while living, is a more realistic goal.

## "Be strong"

Being strong often means hiding emotion. True strength in grief may include crying, asking for help, and admitting it hurts.

## "Time heals all wounds"

Time alone does not heal grief. What you do with time, such as grieving, connecting, remembering, and getting support, matters more.

## What to try instead

- Stay connected through memories, rituals, and letters
- Balance grieving with rest and daily life
- Aim to integrate, not close
- Let others see your grief
- Seek support actively

## Your own wisdom

You know your grief better than any advice. Take what helps and leave the rest. If grief feels unsafe, call or text 988.

title: Self-Compassion in Grief
desc: Grieving people are often harsh with themselves. How self-compassion can ease guilt and help you carry loss.
date: 2026-10-20
---
Grieving people are often hardest on themselves. You should be doing better by now. You should have done more. You should not be crying again. Self-compassion offers a different voice.

## What self-compassion is

Psychologist Kristin Neff describes self-compassion as having three parts: kindness toward yourself, recognizing that suffering is part of being human, and mindful awareness of your feelings without exaggerating or suppressing them.

## Why it matters in grief

Self-criticism adds a second layer of pain on top of grief. Self-compassion can soften that layer, making grief more bearable.

## Practices to try

- **Talk to yourself like a friend.** What would you say to someone you love in your situation?
- **Place a hand on your heart.** A simple physical gesture can signal comfort.
- **Common humanity.** Remind yourself: "Many people feel this. I am not alone."
- **Permission slips.** Write yourself permission to rest, to cry, to laugh.

## Self-compassion and guilt

Self-compassion does not mean ignoring real regrets. It means holding them with kindness. You can acknowledge, "I wish I had called more," and also say, "I was doing my best with what I had."

## It takes practice

If self-compassion feels awkward or false at first, that is normal. Keep practicing.

## When the inner critic is loud

If self-criticism becomes self-hatred or thoughts of harming yourself, please reach out. Call or text 988 any time.

title: Coping With Grief: A Practical Guide
desc: Practical, everyday ways to cope with grief, from the first days through the months and years that follow.
date: 2026-10-23
---
Grief can make everyday life feel impossible. This practical guide offers small, concrete ways to get through the days.

## In the first days

- Let others handle what they can
- Eat something, even if small
- Drink water
- Rest when you can
- Do not make big decisions you do not have to

## In the first months

- Keep simple routines for sleep and meals
- Tell a few people what you need
- Start keeping memories: sayings, stories, details
- Allow yourself time to grieve on purpose
- Allow yourself breaks without guilt

## Managing work

If possible, ask about bereavement leave or flexible hours. Let a trusted colleague know so you have support if grief hits at work.

## Handling hard dates

Plan ahead for birthdays, holidays, and anniversaries. Decide how to spend them, who to be with, and how to honor the person.

## Staying connected

Rituals, letters, memory books, and conversations about the person help keep the bond alive.

## Caring for your body

Gentle movement, time outside, and steady sleep support mood. Limit alcohol, which can worsen sleep and mood.

## Getting support

Groups, counselors, and tools like Murido can offer support. You do not have to do this alone.

## Safety first

If grief brings thoughts of harming yourself, call or text 988 immediately.

title: Acceptance and Remembrance: Two Ideas That Shape Healthy Grief
desc: How acceptance and remembrance work together to help people carry grief while staying connected to the person they lost.
date: 2026-10-26
---
Two ideas sit at the heart of how Murido thinks about grief: acceptance and remembrance. They might sound like opposites. In practice, they work together.

## Acceptance

Acceptance means acknowledging that the death happened and allowing the feelings that come with it, rather than fighting them. It does not mean liking it, approving of it, or being okay with it.

## Remembrance

Remembrance means keeping the person present in your life: their stories, values, sayings, and influence. It is the practical side of continuing bonds.

## Why they need each other

Acceptance without remembrance can feel cold, as if accepting the loss means letting the person go. Remembrance without acceptance can become a way of denying the death. Together, they allow you to say: they are gone, and I still carry them.

## Practices that combine both

- Writing a letter that acknowledges their death and tells them what they still mean to you
- Marking their birthday with a ritual that honors their life
- Using memory prompts while noticing feelings as they arise
- Sharing stories with family that include both laughter and grief

## A balanced grief

People who hold both often describe their grief as bittersweet: painful and loving at the same time. That mix is not a problem to fix. It is what love looks like after loss.

Murido's companions help you sit with grief without being rushed, and its Memory Board and shared memory books help you preserve who they were. If grief ever feels too heavy, call or text 988.
